# Seasonal Allergies (Allergic Rhinitis)

## Overview
Seasonal allergies, also called seasonal allergic rhinitis or "hay fever," happen when the immune system overreacts to airborne substances that are usually harmless, most commonly pollen from trees, grasses, or weeds. Symptoms tend to appear or worsen during specific times of year.

## Common Causes and Triggers
- Tree pollen, typically more common in spring
- Grass pollen, typically more common in late spring and summer
- Weed pollen, typically more common in late summer and autumn
- Mold spores, which can rise in damp or humid conditions
- Higher pollen counts on dry, windy days; lower counts after rain

## Common Symptoms
- Sneezing
- Runny or blocked nose
- Itchy, watery, or red eyes
- Itchy throat or ears
- Postnasal drip and mild cough
- Fatigue, often due to disrupted sleep from congestion

Unlike a cold, seasonal allergy symptoms usually do not include fever and tend to persist for as long as the person is exposed to the trigger, sometimes for weeks.

## Self-Care and General Management
- Checking local pollen forecasts and staying indoors when counts are high, particularly in the morning
- Keeping windows closed during high-pollen periods and using air conditioning with a clean filter
- Showering and changing clothes after being outdoors to remove pollen
- Over-the-counter antihistamines or nasal sprays, used according to package instructions
- Rinsing the nasal passages with saline solution
- Washing bedding regularly during allergy season

## When to See a Doctor
- Symptoms are not controlled by over-the-counter treatment
- Symptoms significantly disrupt sleep, work, or daily activities
- Recurring sinus infections or facial pain/pressure alongside allergy symptoms
- Symptoms of asthma developing alongside allergies, such as wheezing or shortness of breath
- Uncertainty about whether symptoms are allergies versus a cold or another condition, especially if they persist beyond the expected pollen season
